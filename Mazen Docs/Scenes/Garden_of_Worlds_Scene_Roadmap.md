# Garden of Worlds Scene Roadmap
## Six-Scene Prologue and Post-Prologue Plan of Plans

**Status:** Planning document

**Scope:** This roadmap summarizes the six connected introductory scenes and sketches several possible post-Scene-6 arcs. It distinguishes established canon, proposed near-term scenes, future design seeds, latent engine opportunities, and open questions.

---

## Core Progression

The current introductory arc teaches the player the Garden's language through place, movement, and consequence.

1. **Scene 1: Find a path.**
2. **Scene 2: Reveal a hidden surface.**
3. **Scene 3: Navigate a world without a privileged floor.**
4. **Scene 4: Turn the world directly.**
5. **Scene 5: Plan a sequence of turns.**
6. **Scene 6: Discover that worlds remember what was done to them.**

This progression is not only mechanical.

It is philosophical.

The player begins by treating worlds as spaces to cross. By the end of Scene 6, worlds have become persistent, responsive structures that remember route, state, and understanding.

---

# Established Canon

The following elements are treated as canonical for the six-scene prologue.

## The Builders

The Builders do not initially appear as humanoid figures.

They are encountered through grammar:

- recurring layered vessels
- quarter-turn motion
- symbolic state displays
- objects that answer from elsewhere
- portals that respond to completed relationships
- worlds that change configuration rather than merely unlock doors

The player should not yet know whether the vessels, obelisks, orbs, portals, or worlds are machines, Builder interfaces, local cross-sections of a being, or all of these at once.

## World Persistence

Worlds retain state after the player leaves.

Scene 2 remains twisted after the hidden face rotates into view.

Scene 3 remains solved after all six obelisks connect to the central orb.

Scene 4 remains in the configuration produced by the player's first successful direct twist.

Scene 5 remains in its completed live-circuit state.

Scene 6 depends on the player returning to an already changed world.

## Route Matters

Scene 6 introduces route-keyed behavior.

The same world responds differently because the player returns through a new path. This prepares the larger game for route-variant worlds and knowledge-keyed transitions.

## Shape Communicates Meaning, Structure Determines Truth

Exterior worlds may appear planetary, architectural, mechanical, or natural.

Interior worlds may appear like rooms.

Those appearances guide emotion and interpretation, but the underlying topology determines behavior.

# Six-Scene Breakdown

## Scene 1: "The First Clearing"

### Primary Player Lesson

Exploration is the first verb.

The player learns to move, observe, notice repetition, test boundaries, and cross into uncertainty without formal tutorial text.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

The scene introduces a bounded exterior world, facelet-bound props, maze traversal, and portal travel.

The engine is not yet showing its full twist behavior. It is establishing trust in spatial exploration first.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The opening makes the player believe they understand where they are.

It begins with Earthlike sky, dawn, a familiar sun, and a setting that seems physically ordinary. The repeated vessels and final portal quietly undermine that familiarity.

The metaphor is birth into uncertainty.

The world appears simple, but contains a question.

### Builder-Language Development

The layered vessels appear as silent objects at dead ends.

They are not explained.

Their repetition teaches the player that placement matters before the player knows what placement means.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

None.

This is the baseline.

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 1 prepares:

- dead ends that matter
- repeated Builder-associated forms
- portal crossing as chosen commitment
- environmental storytelling without explicit exposition
- the idea that a non-humanoid object may be the first apparent character

---

## Scene 2: "The Four Corners"

### Primary Player Lesson

A hidden surface can contain the way forward.

The player learns that distant devices can share state, that symbols can represent remote conditions, and that a world can rotate to reveal what was physically present but inaccessible.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

Scene 2 introduces persistent device state, reversible toggles, remote status displays, scripted outer-slice rotation, hidden facelets, twist-safe props, and a portal that activates only after a rotated surface settles.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The destination is already there.

It is simply facing the wrong direction.

This reframes progress as reorientation rather than discovery alone. The player does not create the exit; they make the world reveal it.

The sky now contains a blue star about twice the apparent diameter of Scene 1's sun, with no visible moon. A small dark angular world is visible overhead as an unexplained object that later becomes Scene 4.

### Builder-Language Development

The world itself acts as interface.

The central plinth translates distant state into a local symbol. The rotation control turns a completed condition into a physical transformation. The hidden portal assembly teaches that something can be present without being visible.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

Scene 2 depends on the player's willingness to follow a portal and explore another maze after Scene 1.

It builds on the expectation that repeated objects and symbols may matter.

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 2 prepares:

- hidden surfaces
- persistent twist state
- obelisks as stabilizing or focusing structures
- downward travel into a world
- the idea that solved exterior state can later become accessible infrastructure

---

## Scene 3: "The Heart of the World"

### Primary Player Lesson

There is no permanently privileged floor.

The player learns that all six interior faces are walkable, that local up can point toward a shared center, and that a chamber may be a folded world rather than a room.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

Scene 3 introduces interior-world topology, six-face navigation, face crossing, center-oriented gravity or orientation, cumulative symbol matching, central state visualization, and portal placement on a selected valid tile.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The player goes inside the world they changed.

The sky disappears and is replaced by an interior heart: an orb or crystal at the shared center. The world becomes inward, self-contained, and relational.

The metaphor is learning to think without a fixed ground.

### Builder-Language Development

The orb, obelisks, beams, and plinths form a sentence in geometric space.

The Builders still do not speak. They arrange relationships and allow the player to complete them.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

Scene 3 depends on Scene 2's downward portal and its persistent exterior twist.

It also depends on the player having learned to read symbols as relationships rather than labels.

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 3 prepares:

- nonstandard orientation
- central references
- cumulative global state
- portals chosen by the world
- return visits to solved mechanisms
- deeper interiors in which all six local up vectors converge toward a shared center

---

## Scene 4: "The First Turn"

### Primary Player Lesson

The player can directly rotate a world slice.

They also learn that not every attempted twist is legal. Bonds can prevent rotation when a rigid group crosses a slice boundary.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

Scene 4 introduces player-controlled outer-slice twists, refused turns, bonded slices, bond visualization, bond release, player rotation with the slice, and maze rewiring through finalized turns.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The player stops watching worlds change and begins changing them.

The scene is intentionally smaller and quieter after Scene 3. It gives the player space to experiment with the core verb.

The metaphor is permission earned through understanding.

### Builder-Language Development

The layered vessel returns and becomes more active.

It demonstrates a refused rotation, reflects bond state, moves with the slice, and responds when the portal opens. The vessel begins to feel less like scenery and more like a local expression of world logic.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

Scene 4 depends on Scene 3's nebula portal and on the player's understanding of face traversal and spatial orientation.

It also uses the Scene 2 world as a visible celestial reference, reminding the player that previous worlds remain persistent.

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 4 prepares:

- direct twisting as a gameplay verb
- bonds as readable constraints
- props and portals riding facelets
- player orientation changing with a slice
- the need for a fixed sky reference during rotation
- more complex multi-turn planning in Scene 5

---

## Scene 5: "The Broken Meridian"

### Primary Player Lesson

A locally correct twist may be globally wrong.

The player learns to plan a sequence of turns and read live connectivity across a whole world.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

Scene 5 introduces multi-step player-controlled outer-slice twisting, twist-safe channel networks, live flow as puzzle state, temporary versus sustained receiver activation, and a circuit that must remain continuous through several configurations.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The world asks the player to make it agree with itself.

The pale meridian surface suggests an astrolabe, circuit, aqueduct, and living diagram. The player is not opening a door by pressing switches. They are restoring continuity.

The metaphor is coherence.

### Builder-Language Development

Layered vessels now mirror nearby channel connectivity.

Their rings align or misalign as the world state changes. They become increasingly difficult to interpret as mere props.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

Scene 5 depends on Scene 4's direct twist tutorial.

It also depends on the player recognizing persistent world states in the sky, because the Scene 4 world remains visible overhead in its solved configuration.

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 5 prepares:

- complex twist sequences
- live physical feedback replacing schematic UI
- topology as a circuit
- route-keyed return to a familiar world
- puzzles where maintaining previous state matters

---

## Scene 6: "The World Remembered"

### Primary Player Lesson

Returning to a world is not replaying a level.

The player learns that worlds persist, previous twists expose later routes, completed mechanisms become infrastructure, and arrival route can alter what a world offers.

### Primary Engine Capability Introduced

Scene 6 introduces route-keyed return, persistent cross-world state, re-entry into a solved interior world from a new face or route, and portal behavior based on accumulated history.

### Narrative and Metaphorical Function

The world remembers the player's agreement.

Scene 6 converts technical persistence into emotional recognition. The player sees the same blue star, the same rough masonry, the same rotated slice, and the same active interior, but all of it now means something different.

The metaphor is memory as architecture.

### Builder-Language Development

A metal version of the layered vessel appears inside the Scene 3 machinery.

Its proportions, timing, quarter-turn grammar, and impossible reflections match the stone vessels. This becomes the first strong implication that the same Builder presence may present through different materials and functions.

### Dependency on Earlier Scenes

Scene 6 depends on nearly everything before it:

- Scene 2's twisted exterior state
- Scene 3's solved six-obelisk interior
- Scene 4's world state visible in the sky
- Scene 5's route-keyed portal
- the player's growing familiarity with vessels as Builder-associated grammar

### What It Prepares For Later

Scene 6 prepares:

- Act I destinations selected by route
- knowledge-keyed doors
- route-variant worlds
- recognized Builder manifestations
- revisiting worlds as a major design pillar
- puzzle structures based on memory, not inventory

---

# Proposed Near-Term Structure After Scene 6

Scene 6 should feel like the end of the prologue.

The player now understands the fundamental language:

- explore
- observe repetition
- read symbols
- walk unusual surfaces
- twist worlds
- plan twist sequences
- trust persistence
- recognize recurring Builder grammar

After this, the game can open into Act I.

The next destination should not merely be "Scene 7." It should feel like the Garden becoming broader, less linear, and more responsive to the player's history.

---

# Act I Direction: The Garden Opens

## Design Goal

Act I should broaden the player's access to multiple worlds while keeping complexity controlled.

The player has learned enough to navigate a hub-like or route-linked structure, but not enough for the game to demand high-order twist reasoning immediately.

## Possible Arc: The First Conservatory

The route-keyed portal from Scene 6 leads to a conservatory-like world cluster.

This is not a menu hub.

It is a playable world whose portals look like physical exhibits, growths, machines, or rooms. Each portal leads to a different world type that exercises one already learned rule in a new emotional register.

Candidate Act I worlds:

- a world where channels carry sound instead of light
- a world where vessels appear as trees or lanterns
- a world where the sky displays another world's puzzle state
- a world where a portal returns to the same place only if entered from a specific direction
- a small interior world that deepens the no-privileged-floor concept without adding new twist types

## Act I Mechanical Emphasis

Act I should primarily recombine known mechanics:

- outer-slice twists
- bonds
- live connectivity
- route memory
- persistent revisits
- counterpart-in-sky readouts

New mechanics should be introduced sparingly.

The player's main growth in Act I is confidence with the grammar, not raw complexity.

---

# Act II Direction: The Garden Remembers Differently

## Design Goal

Act II should make the player's route, knowledge, and prior world states matter in larger structural ways.

This is where the game can begin to fulfill the engine's stranger promises.

## Route-Variant Worlds

The same apparent destination can resolve to different persistent variants depending on where the player came from.

For example:

- A portal labeled by a familiar symbol leads to World A if entered from the conservatory.
- The same visible world reached from the blue-star exterior becomes World A-route-2, with shared identity but different state.
- Objects, routes, and Builder manifestations change according to origin.

This should not feel like random branching.

It should feel like the world includes the route as part of its identity.

### Design Rule

Route-variant worlds must preserve player trust.

The player should be able to say:

> I reached the same place differently, so the place answered differently.

Not:

> The game secretly swapped maps.

## Knowledge-Keyed Doors

Knowledge-keyed doors change destination or behavior based on what the player has learned rather than inventory items.

Examples:

- A portal that leads to a simple exterior if entered before the player understands vessel grammar, but to a deeper interior after they have recognized the vessel pattern.
- A doorway that opens only after the player has seen the same symbol perform three different functions.
- A world that stops treating a vessel as scenery once the player has activated its counterparts elsewhere.

### Design Rule

Knowledge must be inferable from play.

The game should avoid invisible checklists that feel like hidden achievements.

Knowledge-keyed behavior should be grounded in player-recognizable history.

## Counterpart-in-Sky Puzzles

One world's visible state becomes the readout or control condition for another.

Examples:

- A world in the sky shows which slice must be turned on the current surface.
- A completed circuit on one planet creates a constellation pattern needed elsewhere.
- A world visible overhead rotates whenever the player twists a local counterpart.

### Design Rule

The sky should not become a passive decoration.

If a counterpart is visible, its state should eventually matter.

---

# Act III Direction: Higher-Order World Operations

## Design Goal

Act III should introduce rare, awe-producing operations that feel earned rather than arbitrary.

These are not simply harder puzzles. They are new categories of relationship.

## Middle-Slice Turns

Middle-slice turns are a latent engine capability and should not appear until the player fully understands outer slices.

They change the player's model of what can move.

Outer slices teach:

> The surface can rotate.

Middle slices teach:

> The world's interior structure can shear beneath the surface.

### Introduction Strategy

Introduce middle-slice turns first as a witnessed event.

Then let the player trigger one through a mechanism.

Only later allow direct player control.

The first middle-slice puzzle should be small, slow, and visually clear.

## Multi-Slice or Whole-World Rotations

Multi-slice and whole-world rotations should be rare higher-order events.

They should feel mythic.

Possible uses:

- aligning two worlds across the sky
- rotating an entire world so an interior gravity field changes
- turning multiple slices as a Builder-scale response rather than ordinary player input
- revealing that a familiar hub world has hidden faces of its own

### Design Rule

Do not normalize these events too quickly.

If every scene escalates to larger rotations, wonder collapses into spectacle.

These should mark major act turns or late-game realizations.

## Bonds as Movable, Placeable, or Transferable Objects

Scene 4 introduces bonds as constraints.

Later scenes can make bonds into objects of play.

Variants:

- movable bonds that slide along channels
- placeable bonds that the player uses to intentionally prevent a slice from turning
- transferable bonds that move from one group of cubies to another
- temporary bonds created by light, sound, or Builder vessels

### Design Rule

Bonds should remain readable as physical relationships.

The player should see what is being tied together and why that affects twisting.

---

# Deeper Interior Worlds

Scene 3 introduces a simple 5x5x5 interior.

Later interiors can become stranger while preserving the core rule:

> all six local up vectors converge toward a shared center.

Possible deeper interiors:

- a nested chamber inside the central orb
- an interior whose center is not an object but a void
- a world where obelisks point to several possible centers, forcing the player to identify the true one
- an interior whose six faces represent six remembered routes into the same world
- a chamber where middle-slice turns alter the paths on interior surfaces

The danger is disorientation.

Each deeper interior must provide stable references:

- center object or absence
- repeating audio direction
- face-specific texture
- persistent light beams
- visible route history

---

# First Recognized Builder Manifestation

The first recognized Builder manifestation should occur after the player has enough pattern history to make the realization themselves.

The scene should not introduce a glowing humanoid explaining the Garden.

Instead, the player should encounter a familiar form in an impossible combination:

- a vessel from Scene 1 behaving like the metal vessel from Scene 6
- a portal that answers with the timing of a vessel
- a room whose walls turn in the same rhythm as a Builder-associated object
- a light that appears in several worlds at once
- a world whose weather repeats the quarter-turn grammar

The player realizes:

> These are not separate decorative motifs.
> This is a presence choosing different interfaces for different functions.

## Presentation Rule

Builders choose forms according to function, not appearance.

A guide may be a person.

A warning may be a locked slice.

A memory may be a landscape.

A question may be a door.

The first recognized manifestation should preserve ambiguity while rewarding attention.

---

# Builder Grammar and Recurring Signatures

The Builder grammar should remain consistent enough to recognize but flexible enough to avoid becoming a logo.

## Recurring Signatures

- quarter-turn motion
- impossible reflections
- repeated tones
- objects that answer from elsewhere
- rings or seams aligning with local truth
- symbols that become actions
- forms selected according to function
- surfaces that change meaning when approached from another route
- portals that show the path that made them possible

## Use With Restraint

Do not put every signature in every scene.

Each scene should emphasize one or two signatures clearly.

The pattern becomes powerful because it accumulates over time.

---

# Route Memory as Narrative

Route memory should be treated as more than save-state machinery.

It can express theme:

- what the player did remains real
- returning changes meaning
- knowledge alters access
- the Garden preserves relationships, not just locations
- history is structural

## Design Examples

- A world remembers whether the player first arrived through light, stone, or machinery.
- A portal shows fragments of the route that created it.
- A Builder-associated vessel changes material depending on the route used to reach it.
- A familiar world offers a different interior entrance after being approached from a new origin.
- A future route closes not because of punishment, but because another route has become more true.

## Trust Rule

The player must be able to reconstruct why something changed.

Mystery is healthy.

Opaque state changes are not.

---

# Latent Engine Opportunities

These capabilities should be reserved until the player's conceptual foundation is strong enough.

## Route-Variant Worlds

Use after Scene 6.

The player now understands that route matters, but the first route variants should be modest:

- same world, altered arrival region
- same portal, different local state
- same destination, different Builder manifestation

## Knowledge-Keyed Doors

Use after the player has recognized a pattern, not merely completed a checklist.

Knowledge keys should be tied to:

- observed symbols
- solved relationships
- recognized Builder grammar
- prior routes
- changed worlds visible in the sky

## Middle-Slice Turns

Use after several outer-slice scenes.

The player should first see a middle-slice turn from a stable viewpoint, then later cause one, then eventually control one.

## Multi-Slice and Whole-World Rotations

Use as act-level events or rare Builder-scale interventions.

These should feel like the Garden is revealing a larger grammar, not simply raising difficulty.

## Movable Bonds

Use after the player has internalized bonds as constraints.

Movable bonds can become puzzle objects only when the player already trusts why bonds prevent turns.

## Counterpart-in-Sky Systems

Use whenever a world is visible from another world.

The sky should increasingly become a readable layer of gameplay.

---

# Open Questions

## Puzzle Readability

How much can the player infer from physical channels, symbols, vessels, beams, and sky objects without interface overlays?

The game should aim for diegetic readability, but accessibility options may need optional prompts, diagrams, or high-contrast path indicators.

## Player Disorientation

Interior worlds, slice rotations, and face transitions can become cognitively heavy.

Each scene needs stable references:

- a fixed sky object
- a central orb
- persistent sound direction
- clear material variation between faces
- visible start and destination cues

## Portal Overuse

Portals are currently the main transition device.

The design should vary their meaning:

- outward portal
- downward portal
- nebula portal
- route-keyed portal
- dormant frame from prior completion
- portal that shows the route that made it possible

If portals become generic exits, their mystery will weaken.

## Symbol-Matching Repetition

Scenes 2 and 3 use symbol relationships heavily.

Later scenes should shift emphasis toward:

- live flow
- route memory
- bonds
- sky counterparts
- spatial transformation
- environmental consequences

Symbols should remain part of the Builder language, but not the only puzzle language.

## Maintaining Mystery Without Vagueness

The Builders should become more legible over time, but not over-explained.

The player should learn specific grammar:

- what repeats
- what responds
- what changes with route
- what aligns with twist

Mystery should deepen because the pattern is real, not because the game withholds all answers.

## Growing From Learned Rules

Every new mechanic should grow from an already learned rule.

Preferred pattern:

1. See the phenomenon.
2. Trigger it indirectly.
3. Use it directly.
4. Combine it with earlier rules.
5. Discover its narrative meaning.

This pattern has already emerged:

- Scene 2: witness scripted twist
- Scene 4: control direct twist
- Scene 5: sequence twists
- Scene 6: discover persistent consequences

Future mechanics should follow the same care.

# Recommended Next Design Moves

## Near-Term Deliverables

1. Convert Scenes 1-6 into a unified prologue document with scene numbers, cross-references, and continuity notes.
2. Create a visual symbol sheet for dots, rings, swirl, incomplete square, meridian channels, and route-lines.
3. Define the vessel grammar as a separate design reference.
4. Produce a world-state table showing how Scenes 2-6 persist after completion.
5. Sketch three possible Act I opening worlds and choose one as the first post-prologue destination.

## Prototype Priorities

1. Validate player-controlled outer-slice twists with the player riding the slice.
2. Validate refused twist feedback and bond readability.
3. Validate live channel flow across twistable facelets.
4. Validate persistent return to Scene 2 after prior state changes.
5. Validate route-keyed portal behavior with clearly explainable state dependencies.

## Narrative Priorities

1. Keep the Builders suggestive through function-first manifestations.
2. Avoid explanatory dialogue in the prologue.
3. Let each scene teach one emotional idea alongside one mechanical idea.
4. Make recurring vessels feel increasingly intentional without naming them too early.
5. Preserve the sense that destinations exist before the player knows how to reach them.

---

# Summary

The six-scene prologue moves the player from ordinary exploration to structural memory.

At the beginning, a maze hides a portal.

By the end, a remembered world opens a route because the player returned through a history they created.

That is the foundation of Garden of Worlds:

> The world is not a backdrop.
> It is a changing record of relationships.

The next phase should widen the Garden without abandoning that principle.
